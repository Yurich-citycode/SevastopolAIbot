# The Register of Closed Doors

### Crimea, RWA, and the one percent that is still mine

Written September 26, 2026. Every date, document number and figure below was checked on that day; links to the primary sources are collected at the end. This is not legal advice and not a manual for evading restrictions. It is a map of the terrain, drawn by me, because finished maps do not exist: the people who write about crypto generally do not live where I live, and the people who live here rarely write about crypto.

> "This door was made only for you; now I am going to close it." Kafka, "Before the Law", the last sentence of the parable. I could leave that as a metaphor. In my case it is section 11.3 of a terms of service.

## 1. The line in the user agreement

I will start with a moment I remember physically. I was reading the Service Agreement of a large exchange based in Dubai, breaking nothing, cheating no one, I only wanted to understand which products I could actually use. And in section 11.3, inside the list of excluded jurisdictions, in the same row as North Korea, Cuba and Iran, I found my own name. Not "Russia". Not "the Russian Federation". Separately: Crimea, Donetsk, Luhansk, and, which was genuinely strange to read, Sevastopol, listed as its own word, as if this city held its own legal status inside somebody else's risk register.

A disorienting feeling. You are not on a list of prohibited persons, you are not in an extremism register, you are not a suspect. You are a set of coordinates. You are a row in a table that some lawyer added in 2022 and has been copying from document to document ever since, because compliance demands it. And that row cancels more than every commit I wrote in three years.

There will be a lot of numbers, dates and order numbers in this text. I am deliberately being dry and thorough, because when we talk about sanctions here we swing between slogans ("they are strangling us") and the dead language of lawyers. Both are useless. I needed to understand three things: what exactly the law forbids me, what that means for a specific service, and where there is still a crack I can squeeze through. Honest answer: there is a crack. It is narrow, and it is not about deception. It is about working in places where Crimea is simply not mentioned.

## 2. How the wall is built, and why it is not an attitude

The main misunderstanding I meet even among people I know is: "our side can do nothing, this is political will, tomorrow it will get better". No. There is a specific text, and it works like a lock chamber: first it cuts off the money, then the lawyers, then the hackathons, then hiring.

### 2.1. First contour: an embargo on a region

On December 19, 2014 [Executive Order 13685](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2015-title3-vol1/pdf/CFR-2015-title3-vol1-eo13685.pdf) was signed, "Blocking Property of Certain Persons and Prohibiting Certain Transactions with Respect to the Crimea Region of Ukraine". Section 1(a) prohibits, among other things, four acts, and all four stand above my biography. The first: any new investment in the Crimea region by a US person, wherever that person is located. The second: exportation, reexportation, sale or supply, from the United States or by a US person, of any goods, services or technology to Crimea, directly or indirectly. The third: importation into the United States of any goods, services or technology from Crimea, also directly or indirectly. The fourth: any approval, financing, facilitation or guarantee by a US person of a transaction by a foreign person that would be prohibited if a US person performed it.

That last one deserves to be underlined. It makes the guilty party not only the person who pays me, but the employee inside an American company who merely approved the payment. Part 589 speaks about services as broadly as language allows, with no list of permitted or forbidden categories: the rule accompanying §589.207 states that the prohibition on supplying services covers services performed "on behalf of a person in the Crimea region of Ukraine or where the benefit of such services is otherwise received in the Crimea region of Ukraine", which is my case precisely: the work is done remotely and the benefit is received here; §589.208 bans importation "directly or indirectly", through any third party at all. The exceptions in this body of law are narrow and humane: legal and accounting services supporting human-rights work and media, mail, telecommunications, internet-based communications, NGO activity, journalism (see [OFAC's explanatory note to the rule of May 2, 2022](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022-05-02/2022-09371/ukraine-russia-related-sanctions-regulations)).

The practical conclusion for me, a developer and an RWA researcher, is hard and single-use. If I do work from Sevastopol for a company in the US, I am not the violator, I am the object. But the prohibited transaction is precisely their side of the deal, accepting my work as an import of services from Crimea. Not my act. Theirs. That is why the refusal I receive is almost never phrased as "you may not". It is phrased as "our policy is not to do business with that region", and standing behind that policy is a fine that somebody has already paid.

### 2.2. Second contour: why every exchange answers me with the same sentence

October 2022. [OFAC and FinCEN fine Bittrex](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1006): $24 million on the OFAC side and $29 million on the FinCEN side. The reason that caught me specifically: the exchange had not blocked transactions by people who, according to IP data and the addresses in its own KYC files, were located in Crimea, Cuba, Iran, Sudan and Syria. Value of the transactions: $263,451,600.13. Number of alleged violations: about 116,000. The key construction in the release: "based on internet protocol address information and physical address information collected about each customer at onboarding, Bittrex had reason to know". Meaning it is enough that a company *could have known*, from its own data, where the client sits. Not "wanted to know". Not "checked". Could have.

This is why any service with a real legal department, from Bybit to OKX, from DoraHacks to a payout platform, writes Crimea into its rules as a separate word. Not out of malice. Because somebody already paid $53 million over $263 million of that volume. I am, in effect, paying with my career for a fine a Bellevue-based exchange paid. That is the most honest illustration of how collective punishment works in the compliance era: the cost of an error is calculated across a whole region, so access is closed across a whole region.

OKX describes this plainly in its Risk and Compliance Disclosure (section 3): accounts registered in "occupied regions of Ukraine" are blocked, and what decides the matter is not only the resident's declaration but the document itself. Their phrasing is simple: paperwork issued in a blocked location halts the account before any funds move. A paper issued in a blocked location stops the account before money moves. Reading that, I felt the field narrow: it is not only the registration stamp in my passport, it is that any certificate, statement, diploma or reference issued here carries the same prefix as my address.

### 2.3. Third contour: services "on the other side" are forbidden too

The year 2024 finished off what I had naively considered open country. On June 12 [OFAC issued a Determination](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/added/2024-06-12), "Prohibition on Certain Information Technology and Software Services", under E.O. 14071, in force from September 12, 2024. From that date a US person may not supply Russia with IT consulting and design services and with software design services, which, per OFAC's guidance and the UN CPC codes 83131 and 83141, includes designing the structure of an application and writing its code. Yes, this is about services delivered *into* Russia, not *out of* it. But the effect on the labor market was the one I had watched from the inside: American companies stopped taking contractors from Russia altogether, because inside a compliance policy nobody draws a line between "he writes code for us" and "he somehow participates in IT services for a Russian jurisdiction". They simply insured themselves.

Right next to it lies the 2022 ban on registering a company for me. On May 8, 2022 OFAC determined that from June 7, 2022 US persons may not provide three categories of services into Russia: accounting (including tax planning and filing, FAQ 1068), trust and corporate formation services (incorporation, including finding a nominee director, providing a registered address, registered agent services; FAQ 1063 clarifies that it does not matter whether the company is new or an existing one being serviced), and management consulting (including executive search and vetting, FAQ 1064). And here is what matters for understanding the shape of my closure: "person located in the Russian Federation" in [FAQ 1058](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/6626) is not only someone physically inside Russia but also anyone "ordinarily resident" there. The address in my registration is my jurisdiction.

One more precise stroke. As far back as July 30, 2015 OFAC published a [Crimea Sanctions Advisory](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/8676/download?inline=) on how evasion looks in the data. The first typology named: deliberately omitting the Crimea address in SWIFT payment messages. In other words, "deleting" Crimea from paperwork is itself a red flag that companies have been warned about for eleven years. Which is also the answer to the commenters who write "your only problem is that you type Sevastopol". No. The problem is that I am from Sevastopol.

### 2.4. Fourth contour: the EU reached for the passport, not just the address

Until 2025 I had built my strategy on a gap. The US was fully closed, but the EU had never imposed a comprehensive embargo on Crimea as a territory, only targeted sectoral bans. It looked as if a German or Dutch firm could hire a contractor physically in Crimea, because the EU does not prohibit "services from Crimea" in the same volume, and money could in theory be routed through third countries. Naive.

On October 23, 2025 the Council of the EU adopted the [19th sanctions package](https://finance.ec.europa.eu/news/eu-adopts-19th-package-sanctions-against-russia-2025-10-23_en). According to the analyzes by [Ashurst Perkins Coie](https://www.ashurstperkinscoie.com/en/insights/overview-of-the-eus-19th-sanction-package/) and [Cassidy Levy Kent](https://www.cassidylevy.com/news/the-eu-adopts-19th-sanctions-package/), Article 5b of Regulation 833/2014 was rewritten so that EU persons are prohibited from providing **all crypto-asset services** (as defined by MiCA, Regulation 2023/1114), the issuance of payment instruments, acquiring, payment initiation and e-money issuance, not only to residents of Russia but to **Russian citizens**, wherever they are. In force from November 25, 2025; from January 18, 2026 a further ban on Russians owning, controlling or holding positions in EU crypto wallet, account and custody providers; and from August 25, 2026 the ownership and control prohibition was extended to all MiCA services, including crypto advisory and portfolio management (overview: [crypto.news, August 2026](https://cryptonews.net/news/legal/33310812/)).

Read that again and feel the scale. Before, I was cut by my address. Now I am cut by my passport, across the entire territory of the EU. Nothing changes if I move to Tbilisi, Belgrade or Yerevan: to a European CASP I remain a "Russian national", and under the letter of Article 5b no crypto services may be provided to me. The [Commission's FAQ](https://sanctionsnews.bakermckenzie.com/eu-issues-new-faq-guidance-on-the-provision-of-payments-services-under-the-eu-russia-sanctions/) confirms the trigger: (i) Russian citizens, (ii) residents of Russia, (iii) legal entities established in Russia. The only window mentioned in the same FAQ: people holding a long-stay national D visa, legally registered in an EU member state, fall outside the ban. Which means real European residency removes the block. Not "moving to a neighbouring region". Not "I am here temporarily". A genuine residence permit, a registry, a registration. That is when it becomes clear what the whole ordeal is worth paying for.

### 2.5. Fifth contour: doors do not open on their own

I want to fix the state of play as of today, because every conversation that begins with "when the war ends everything will open" breaks against the calendar of the past year alone.

On April 23, 2026 the EU adopted the [20th package](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026-04-23/russia-s-war-of-aggression-against-ukraine-20th-round-of-stern-eu-sanctions-hits-energy-military-industrial-complex-trade-and-financial-services-including-crypto/): a full sectoral ban on transactions with providers and platforms established in Russia, including the decentralised venues used to circumvent the restrictions, a ban on operations in RUBx, and any support for the digital ruble.

On July 23, 2026, the [21st package](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/07/european-union-adopts-21st-package-against-russia-and-parallel-sanctions-on-belarus), per [Reuters](https://live.euronext.com/en/financial-news/factbox-whats-eus-21st-package-sanctions-against-russia): 218 new designations (170 entities and 48 individuals), asset freezes on 94 Russian financial institutions, 33 banks cut from SWIFT, a ban on transactions with 14 crypto platforms in third countries (Georgia, Panama, the UAE, the Marshall Islands and Belarus are in the list), and, most interesting of all, a new legal basis for banning any dealings with every crypto operator of any third country that the Council finds complicit in circumvention.

Meanwhile, on September 16, 2026 the US House passed [H.R. 5334, the "Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026"](https://www.fieldfisher.com/en/services/international-trade/trade-sanctions-blog/uk-eu-and-us-sanctions-on-russia), with two mechanisms: country-level tariffs and sanctions on named individuals and companies in energy.

And now the part that speaks to the voice inside me that wants to believe. On September 14, 2026 the Council of the EU **failed** to renew the asset-freeze regulation: Slovakia demanded that Usmanov and Fridman be removed, France offered a compromise, governments were given a week, and on September 21 they were still haggling. This is, incidentally, the only good news of the year: the system is not monolithic, it is patchy, decisions are pushed through vetoes, and one conclusion follows, which I did not draw immediately. The regime does not rest on a consensus about "punishing Crimea". It rests on the weakest link in the procedure. So it is conservative and will only harden at the edges (the 19th and 21st packages show that), while nobody has built any political momentum toward relief. Waiting for access to be granted to me is pointless. Planning a life in which access is not granted is useful.

## 3. The mechanics of refusal, or what "no" looks like from inside

"No" almost never arrives as a letter. It arrives as an interface. The "Continue" button is active, the form is filled, the spinner turns, and then the status reads: Verification failed: region not supported. No explanation. I spent an evening collecting the exact wording in one place, because I needed to stop believing this was bad luck. It is not bad luck, it is a line in documents, and it is identical everywhere.

Exchanges. Bybit, Service Agreement section 11.3 (current summary by [Datawallet, August 2026](https://www.datawallet.com/crypto/bybit-restricted-countries)) keeps four exclusions, right next door to each other: Crimea, Donetsk, Luhansk and Sevastopol. Separately. Russian-language explainers for the rest of the country put it like this: Russia is not blocked, but Crimea, Donetsk, Luhansk and Sevastopol are, while KYC is mandatory and a Russian internal or foreign passport is accepted. So verification from Sevastopol technically passes with a document issued somewhere else, but the mere fact of a local registration in the address field turns the account into an anomaly that compliance will close at the first opportunity.

Freelance platforms. [Upwork](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/211067778-Who-s-eligible-to-join-and-use-Upwork) states that operations in Russia and Belarus have been suspended since May 1, 2022, and adds, separately, that US law limits Upwork in doing business with persons or organizations who are located or traveling in restricted regions. The word "traveling" is not decoration. If you go to a conference and come back, you are "in a restricted area" at exactly the moment your session is active.

Payment and employment layers. Payoneer and Wise: Wise stopped transfers to Russia on [February 28, 2022](https://reuters.com/article/ukraine-crisis-russia-sanctions-payments/update-1-payments-company-wise-suspends-money-transfer-business-in-russia-idINL1N2V324X), and had never served Crimea. Russian overviews from 2026 ([avo.cards](https://avo.cards/blog/kak-frilanseru-poluchat-oplatu-iz-za-rubeja-2026/index.php)) describe the state of things as "technically working, but a direct payout to a Russian card is impossible, you need a foreign account", meaning the whole construction rests on the one account nobody will open for me where I live.

Employer-of-record platforms. Deel, the global instrument that says "we will onboard you as a contractor or employee in any country", stopped taking new clients with Russian contractors back in 2022, and on May 27, 2025, according to The Information (retold in a [market overview](https://mishkadru.livejournal.com/12570.html)), it also stopped payroll for new employees sitting in Russia. Remote.com explicitly does not cover Russia or Belarus with its Contractor Management. So even the 90 or 150 countries these platforms "cover" contain a hole shaped exactly like me.

American banks phrase it more bluntly than anyone. Here is a standard clause from a Bank Services Agreement of a large US bank ([Truist](https://www.truist.com/content/dam/truist-bank/us/en/documents/disclosures/banking/bank-services-agreement-privacy-notice.pdf)): if you or any account owner has ever been in a comprehensively sanctioned jurisdiction, including Crimea, Donetsk and Luhansk, you will not use, access, or *attempt* to access the account. Not "violate". "Attempt". That is the level of suspicion attached to me: I am dangerous not through actions but through geographic intent.

Why list all of this instead of saying "they do not like me". Because after I gathered those twelve refusals into one document, I felt lighter. Truly. It became clear that I am not a weak candidate, not short on English, not short on a portfolio. I pass every criterion except the one you cannot grind. That is not consolation, it is a diagnosis. What follows is work on what has been diagnosed.

## 4. Chain one: getting hired

I will start with how hot the market is that I am excluded from, because what matters here is not envy but the size of the loss.

RWA tokenization in 2026 is the fastest growing hiring segment in the industry. According to the [State of Web3 Hiring 2026](https://gm.careers/blog/state-of-web3-hiring-2026) review, companies such as Ondo, Centrifuge and Securitize are growing, BlackRock and Franklin Templeton are building their own tokenization rails, and this sector has created thousands of engineering, product and legal roles. Rates: a mid-level Solidity developer earns $140,000-190,000 base and $180,000-280,000 total, a senior $180,000-250,000 base and $250,000-400,000 total, staff and lead up to $350,000-500,000; base salaries rose 10-15 percent in a year precisely because traditional finance money arrived. The competition is savage at the same time: engineering openings receive [more than 450 applicants](https://eakdigital.com/crypto-recruiting-how-web3-companies-hire-top-talent-2026/), while demand for web3 roles grew 395 percent over the period.

Then you open real job postings, and you see the precise place where you fall out. On [web3.career, remote tokenization roles](https://web3.career/remote+tokenization-jobs), there is "Expansion Manager, RWA Tokenization, 100% Remote" from Tether at $102,000-117,000, and under it the list of hiring locations: Warsaw, Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Milan, Madrid, Bangkok, Ho Chi Minh City, São Paulo, Zurich. The role is remote, the money is paid from a jurisdiction where the company can legally onboard you. That is precisely what I do not have, and will not have while I am here.

Or "Senior Product Manager, Tokenization" at Coinbase: remote, $207,000-244,000, but the requirements include being "authorized to work" in the employer's country of tax residence.

Why not just "onboard me as a contractor and pay me in USDT". You can, and tens of thousands of Russian-speaking developers do it; I know these people personally. But look at what the hiring market itself says. A [2026 recruiting overview](https://eakdigital.com/crypto-recruiting-how-web3-companies-hire-top-talent-2026/) describes the "practical approach" for companies as *entity structure planning*, setting up legal entities in the US, the EU, Singapore and Dubai "to simplify onboarding and compensation". In other words: normal hiring is a chain of "you are in a country where we have an entity, or where our partners can onboard you", and every link in that chain (immigration, bank, compliance, accounting) checks one variable: the address. RWA job postings require, in 99 percent of cases:

1. The right to work in the employer's country or in that of its EOR partner (and EOR providers do not operate in Russia).
2. Contractor KYC: passport, proof of address, sometimes a utility bill from the last three months. OKX writes this in plain text.
3. Tax paperwork: a W-8BEN, and for a payout platform an account in a supported country.
4. No exposure to sanctioned banks in your statements (CIS banks check this, it is an [explicit item on their compliance checklist](https://www.andersen-bs.ru/stati/kak-otkryit-schet-v-banke-stranyi-sng-grajdaninu-rossii/)).
5. And above all: the absence of any obligation for *them* to block you. That is the one requirement I can never satisfy, because it is not an HR rule, it is US law.

So I reduced the fork to this. Getting hired from Crimea is not a task of "becoming stronger". It is a task of "changing jurisdiction", with a career as a side effect, not the other way round. Everything else, the "I will finish learning, ship three more projects, and then they will hire me because talent always finds a way", is self-deception with a pleasant taste. I tried it.

There is also good news, and it is real: inside the industry there is a whole layer of work where the address is checked weakly, because there is little to pay with and nothing to onboard you through. I write about it separately in the section on grants, bounties and hackathons, and there I give honest numbers too, because the ceiling of that layer is roughly $10,000-30,000 a year, not $250,000. These are different games and they must be kept apart, otherwise you start comparing yourself with yourself, only with a different address, and you lose your mind.

## 5. Chain two: register a company, open an account, keep living

"Register a foreign company and work as a B2B contractor" is the most popular advice I get online. I am going to take it apart screw by screw, because at the level of screws it falls apart, and in places it turns out to be the opposite of what it looks like.

### 5.1. The United States: closed by law, not by policy

A Delaware LLC through a self-service provider is what an ordinary remote worker sets up in an evening. I cannot, and not because the provider is hostile. Since June 7, 2022 [US persons are prohibited from providing incorporation services into Russia](https://www.williamsmullen.com/insights/news/legal-news/ofac-prohibits-providing-accounting-trustcorporate-formation-and), including finding directors and secretaries, registered agent services, nominee participants and a registered address (FAQ 1063). That is, word for word, "trust and corporate formation services". So any American service that registers a company for me becomes the violator itself. The same goes for accounting, which is everything an LLC needs to be maintained: tax filing is an accounting service, FAQ 1068.

So: the United States, as a jurisdiction for my company, is switched off not by my passport but by their own criminal and administrative risk. It is exactly as painful as it is absurd: I cannot use American incorporation because the United States forbade Americans from incorporating me an American company.

### 5.2. Britain: open, but with a lock I cannot get through

A UK company theoretically requires no British residency: one individual director, one shareholder, a registered office in the UK, incorporation through Companies House for £50 or through a formation agent for £149-549.

But since November 18, 2025, under the ECCTA 2023, identity verification is mandatory for every director and PSC, including foreign ones (overviews by [HopeTex](https://hopetex.co.uk/blog/companies-house-identity-verification-for-directors-uk-overseas/) and [OpenA European Company](https://www.openaeuropeancompany.com/blog/form-a-uk-company-as-a-non-resident/)). And here is what this regime does to a Russian (and to my type of case), according to the detailed country guide by [Verifying Directors](https://verifydirectors.com/blog/overseas-director-identity-verification-companies-house): the biometric flow through GOV.UK One Login does not accept Russian and Belarusian passports, for sanctions reasons. What remains is the paid route through an Authorised Corporate Service Provider, the ACSP, where a human verifies you on a video call. Then comes the part that justifies writing texts like this one: if the system geolocates you during verification into a sanctioned country, verification fails at screening. The guidance explicitly advises not to use a VPN during the check, because a geographic mismatch is itself a red flag, and some ACSPs refuse clients with "strong residential ties" to Russia or Belarus altogether, requiring enhanced due diligence.

My conclusion: a British company is available to me only as a second step, after legal residency in a third country, and only with a properly assembled "center of vital interests" pack. Trying to "verify from Sevastopol" is not a clever move, it is an application for a refusal and for a blacklist entry with the provider.

### 5.3. Estonia: the e-Residency that no longer exists for me

A private wound, because eight years ago this was the most elegant story in the world: a digital signature, an OÜ, 0 percent tax on undistributed profits, access to European services. [The Estonian government stopped accepting e-Residency applications from Russian and Belarusian citizens on March 11, 2022](https://rus.err.ee/1608524723/grazhdane-rf-i-belarusi-bolshe-ne-smogut-stat-je-rezidentami-jestonii), rejected applications already in the queue, and placed issued cards under enhanced supervision with the possibility of revocation; at the time, crypto-focused e-residents reported mass notifications that their certificates were being suspended. According to [2026 overviews](https://legaladdressinestonia.com/blog/what-is-estonian-e-residency/) the ban still stands, and the narrow exception applies to holders of long-term EEA residence. Meanwhile the program itself, by a 2026 review, is 135,000+ e-residents, 39,000+ companies and a record €124.9 million contributed to the Estonian budget in 2025, and it keeps working for everybody else. The feeling of watching a mechanism run with you switched out of it is hard to name in one word. There is astonishment (it actually worked!), grief, anger, and the understanding that €124 million does not smell of anyone's registration stamp.

### 5.4. Georgia, Armenia, Serbia, the UAE: four countries that have not closed

Georgia. You can open a company or a sole proprietorship while in the country on the one-year visa-free stay; 183 days of physical presence gives tax residency, and the virtual zone for IT services to foreign clients is 0 percent corporate profit tax (per the [Unwildered guide, February 2026](https://www.unwildered.co.uk/legal-blog/easiest-stay-georgia-residency-russians-2026)). But: from March 1, 2026 a work permit is mandatory for any foreigner carrying out paid activity, self-employment included, with a transition period to January 1, 2027, and in March 2026 the real-estate threshold for a residence permit was raised from $100,000 to $150,000. And the main thing I pulled out of live threads in the relevant communities in [2026](https://www.reddit.com/r/Sakartvelo/comments/1s46n35/georgia_ie_setup_is_residence_permit_now/): by one reading an IE needs both a work permit and a residence permit, by another only the work permit, the rules are new and crooked, and accountants give different answers. Banks: remote account opening for Russians was [closed completely in 2024](https://vc.ru/money/2257743-kak-rossiyanam-otkryt-schyot-v-gruzii), the application form makes you list "countries the transfers come from", and Credo remains, per overviews, the only bank that opens an account without extra paperwork.

Armenia. A non-resident can open an account, but you must prove your center of interests: a residence permit, or an employment contract plus long-term lease, and at larger banks such as Ameriabank the compliance review of the origin of your income makes you start distrusting yourself. Opening an account costs 20,000-25,000 AMD, and the same place carries a [reminder about Russian filings](https://www.klerk.ru/blogs/EasypayWorld/699016/): a fine of 4,000-5,000 rubles for failing to notify the tax office of the account and 2,000-3,000 rubles (up to 20,000 on a repeat) for failing to report movement of funds.

Serbia. The "digital nomad visa" program was never adopted there ([immigrantinvest overview, April 2026](https://immigrantinvest.com/ru/blog/serbia-residence-permit-guide/), [mirsatori](https://mirsatori.com/ru/blog/vnzh-serbii-dlya-frilanserov)), so residence is obtained through a d.o.o. or a sole proprietorship: "privremeni boravak" for up to three years, a state duty of 22,700 RSD plus proof of roughly 1,500-2,000 euros in the bank, permanent residence after three years. Crypto regulation exists since 2021: holding and trading need no license, but a service provider needs approval from the Securities Commission or the NBS, and a foreigner needs a Serbian entity or branch for that ([VertexLaw, 2026 overview](https://vertexlaw.io/ru/криптолицензия-для-платформ-обмена-цифровых-активов/)).

The UAE. For a non-resident individual the only account available is a savings one, a full current account is for companies registered in the country; Wio, Mashreq and FAB require an Emirates ID or a residence visa and a minimum balance (overviews: [visasam, May 2026](https://visasam.ru/samotur/finansy/schet-za-granicei.html)). But the UAE is the only country in this list where a Dubai address in your KYC removes almost every question, because you stop being "a person from a region" for everyone except US banks.

And still, after running this whole gauntlet, I hit the thing that made me read Part 589 in the first place. The US embargo is attached to where the *person* is, not to his tax number. Registering a firm in Serbia while living in Sevastopol gives you an entity whose beneficiary is "ordinarily resident" in Russia, which under [FAQ 1058](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/6626) is the very variable on which payments, accounts and contracts burn. Incorporation without relocation is cosmetic surgery on yourself. It changes nothing except reporting.

## 6. Chain three: money, that is, the real physics

There is an old joke in the crypto scene: "in crypto we trust, but invoice we must pay". I would rephrase: money is not a matter of trust, money is a matter of whose bank will clear the payment. So my whole analysis reduces to three variables: where I physically am, who pays, and through which pipe.

### 6.1. The legal pipes open to a person from Russia, and why they are not quite mine

The intermediary market for paying freelancers in 2026 looks like this ([buyhold overview, July 2026](https://buyhold.ru/zarubezhnyy-frilans-rynok/), [digitalbroccoli review](https://digitalbroccoli.com/ru/payments-freelance/)): Solar Staff (0 percent on the contractor, a 3.5-6 percent markup on the client, personal income tax withheld if you lack status), EasyStaff Invoice (from 2.5-6 percent, payout in rubles or in crypto), Mellow, Karta (a dollar account). Plus, as noted, Deel, which requires an address outside Russia and can pay in USDC.

This works for my colleagues in Kazan and Novosibirsk. My problem with this market is structural: all of these services are either American or run through American processing layers, so they inherit Upwork's wording about being "located or traveling in restricted areas". None of them lists Crimea in a price table, and none explains what to do if you are simultaneously a resident of Russia and a resident of nothing else. When I asked support directly, the answer was in the genre of "please confirm your country of incorporation and residential address". They do not accept a Sevastopol address, because accepting it means answering questions nobody wants to answer.

### 6.2. Crypto as a channel, and the new Russian reality from September 1, 2026

Here I should stop, because in the last three weeks something happened at home that I did not believe at first.

On August 4, 2026 [Federal Law No. 282-FZ, "On Digital Currency and Digital Rights", was signed](https://www.cbr.ru/press/event/?id=32719). From September 1, 2026 the Bank of Russia starts building registers of market participants and issuing licenses. From July 1, 2027, residents' crypto transactions must go only through intermediaries in the central bank's register. Non-qualified investors may buy the most liquid coins after a test, within 300,000 rubles a year per intermediary; qualified investors have no monetary cap. Paying domestically with crypto remains forbidden. Exporters and importers, that is corporate residents and sole proprietors, get a permanent (not experimental, as since September 2024) regime of crypto settlements on foreign trade contracts, with no limits on amount and any type of wallet. Holding crypto abroad requires notifying the tax service. And buying crypto peer-to-peer with a Russian card becomes, per lawyers' analyzes ([pravorf, July 2026](https://pravorf.ru/blog/novoe-regulirovanie-kriptovalyut-s-1-iyulya-2026)), a violation for buyer and seller alike from September 1, 2026, because the Rosfinmonitoring blocking mechanism switches on. Transactions without a licensed intermediary, in lawyers' assessment, lose judicial protection.

Read that a second time and notice the irony that made my jaw tighten. In the country where I live they have just created a legal digital-currency market with crypto settlements in foreign trade, with infrastructure, a register and investor testing. And I, by my registration, am not a subject of this market but a risk factor for any counterparty in it. The shop is open to me, the till will not take my passport. That feeling is not about "sanctions", it is about how life is assembled: you are allowed to *own*, you are not allowed to *be a counterparty*.

### 6.3. The hidden layer: why even "paying for a service" is a separate project

Small things nobody describes because they look domestic. I need GitHub Copilot, ChatGPT, a VPS, domains, blockchain API keys, paid courses. Stripe, behind a good part of that infrastructure, does not work in Russia, and in Crimea it does not work especially hard. Subscriptions paid with a Russian card go through once in a while, because acquiring runs through banks that are themselves blocked: RNKB sits in OFAC's SDN list under the program code UKRAINE-EO13685, that is, under the exact order that closes my market; Promsvyazbank was added to the SDN list on February 22, 2022 under E.O. 14024; Vozrozhdenie Bank, renamed VGBR, has been under European restrictions since 2014. Every time I pay for an analytics terminal "through a friend's card", I am not saving twenty dollars, I am borrowing somebody else's risk. And one day that debt is repaid from somebody else's account.

## 7. Chain four: hackathons, or where they cannot find me

Historically the hackathon was the main social elevator in this industry: no diplomas, no interviews, 48 hours, and either you shipped it or you did not. I want to take this place apart in detail, because this is where I spent the most emotional strength, and this is where most of my "one percent" lives.

### 7.1. The clause that kills ninety percent of the options

Devpost, the largest hackathon platform, has a template section 3, "Eligibility", which organizers copy almost verbatim. Here it is in the rules of the [Elasticsearch Agent Builder Hackathon](https://elasticsearch.devpost.com/rules): the hackathon is closed to (I list as in the text) "Brazil, Quebec, Belarus, Cuba, Iran, North Korea (DPRK), Sudan, Syria, Russia, including the Ukrainian territories annexed by Russia (The Crimea region of Ukraine, The Donetsk People's Republic (DNR), The Luhansk People's Republic (LNR), Kherson and Zaporizhzhia)", and also to anyone to whom a sponsor may not lawfully award a prize, and to anyone in a destination prohibited by the Export Administration Regulations.

Here is the same clause in the [Build with Gemini XPRIZE rules](https://www.geminixprize.com/rules): residents of countries where US law forbids participation or the receipt of a prize are excluded, including "Russia, Crimea, Cuba, Iran, North Korea" and any other OFAC-designated place. And here it is again in the [Nebius x NVIDIA Global AI Hackathon](https://tierones.io/opportunities/nebius-nvidia-ai-2026), where Crimea appears in one list with Cuba, Iran and North Korea.

I spent a month reading the rules of thirty hackathons. Result: Crimea is named with the word "Crimea" in about a third of them (usually next to Russia as a whole), and in the rest the restriction hides in the payment layer or in the requirement that a participant "must not be a resident of an OFAC-restricted jurisdiction", which for me means precisely the same thing. When I had squeezed those thirty rulebooks into a single table I sat down on a chair and laughed. Because there is not even contempt in the wording. There is "The Crimea region of Ukraine". Legal prose describing me as a territory rather than as a person who wants to enter an agent-development contest.

### 7.2. What is still open, and it is not consolation but a working tool

Online hackathons on DoraHacks. The platform is Asian in origin and not OFAC-centric, and events live there calmly with no country list in the rules: the [Casper Hackathon 2026](https://dorahacks.io/hackathon/casper-hackathon-2026), virtual, prize pool $25,000; [Mantle's "The Turing Test Hackathon 2026"](https://devhub.mantle.xyz/) with over $120,000 in prizes and a total value above $223,000, registered through DoraHacks and HackQuest, with Demo Day streamed on July 2-3, 2026. Payouts there are usually in crypto, to a wallet, with no "where do you live".

ETHGlobal: their general rules contain no country ban, and that is true. But to travel to Paris, Bangkok or Tokyo I need a visa, which I do not have (below). To receive a prize I need a payout, and it goes either through a payment provider with sanctions screening or in USDC to an address that is instantly run through an AML policy.

The practical conclusion in one line: hackathons that pay in crypto and do not ask for a passport are not "a loophole for the poor", they are the only method that is legal for everyone to make an impression on the industry. Everything else is just a prettier form of refusal.

### 7.3. On visas, because without this the hackathon talk turns into fiction

There is a list of things I learned about leaving. It is short and very concrete.

The document. Under [Implementing Decision (EU) 2023/1061](https://www.envoyglobal.com/news-alert/european-commission-reports-new-schengen-restrictions-on-russian-non-biometric-passports/), confirmed by the Commission's update of August 31, 2026, Russian travel documents issued in Crimea, Sevastopol, Donetsk, Luhansk, Kherson, Zaporizhzhia and Kharkiv regions, Abkhazia and South Ossetia are not accepted either for issuing a Schengen visa or for crossing the border. This rule sits above the national policies of EU states. According to an [August 2026 overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_passport) the United States likewise does not recognize Russian passports issued in Crimea and Sevastopol after 2014. So: if my foreign passport was "born" in Sevastopol, it is not a document but a souvenir. The first real action in my plan was not "learn Solidity", it was "reissue my foreign passport in another region of Russia", which, luckily, is legal, passes normally, and costs about 5,000 rubles in state duty.

The country of filing. Since September 6, 2025 the State Department [barred Russians](https://caliber.az/en/post/us-designates-astana-warsaw-for-russian-visa-applications) from applying for non-immigrant visas anywhere except their country of citizenship or legal permanent residence: for Russians that means Astana and Warsaw, for Belarusians Vilnius and Warsaw, for Ukrainians Warsaw and Krakow. The fee is $185, and the wait for a slot runs, per [practice guides](https://vc.ru/global_payments_easycrds/2736592-kak-poluchit-vizu-v-ssha-dlya-rossiyan-v-2026-godu-v-astane-i-varshave), from two weeks to six months; the State Department itself warns that filing outside your place of residence or legal permanent residence means a markedly longer wait. Schengen, in the 2024 statistics [compiled by HelloSafe from DG HOME data](https://hellosafe.com/schengen-visa/rejection), is 606,594 applications from Russians and a 7.5 percent refusal rate. A pretty number, if you forget that Poland and the three Baltic states are effectively closed to tourist travel from Russia in 2026 by national restrictions, and that since October 2025 the EU's border is running EES, which photographs your face and takes your fingerprints.

The last paragraph matters not as a complaint but as arithmetic. I sit in a region where a regional emergency regime was introduced on June 26, 2026, where according to [data from the grid operator](https://crimea.ria.ru/20260719/na-vsey-territorii-kryma-deystvuet-rezhim-ogranicheniya-elektrosnabzheniya-1157769595.html) power restrictions were in force across the whole territory on July 19, 2026, where the capacity deficit persists into September 2026, and where in summer the peninsula introduced limits on selling petrol to private drivers. Competing in an international hackathon, for me, is a sport where you race people who have electricity and you have three hours a day without it. And that, not the sanctions, is the most unjust part of this story: sanctions take away the future, a de-energized city takes away the ability to assemble it right now.

## 8. Chain five: foundation grants, where a small black box labeled OFAC sits

The grant economy in crypto is real: the Ethereum Foundation, through the Ecosystem Support Program, hands out small grants of $5,000 to $50,000 and up to $200,000 and more for infrastructure, without requiring a legal entity (per a [2026 conditions overview](https://www.grantchain.eu/grants/ethereum-foundation-esp)), and "anyone worldwide" is formally what is stated. The Solana Foundation, through Superteam Earn, hands out microgrants from $1 to $10,000 with a philosophy printed on their site in plain text: "Proof of Work > Credentials", and "Anyone is welcome to apply, given they have enough Proof of Work".

That phrase about proof of work is more important than it looks. It is the one place in my life where the industry voluntarily gave up checking passports. And at the same time it is the place where they bring the check back from the other side.

What I found when I started reading the rules line by line.

First: Crimea appears in the lists literally. The [SNS ID Grant Program](https://docs.sns.id/collection/programs/grant-program) says that to claim a grant you must not be a user from sanctioned geographies, and lists, among others, "the Crimea region of Ukraine", along with the United States, Thailand, Zimbabwe and some dozen more. Formally this is not about me, formally it is about "a region". In fact it is about me.

Second: the optimistic idea that "DAOs do not ask for documents" broke on [Optimism Retro Funding](https://community.optimism.io/citizens-house/rounds/retropgf-6). Their text is plain: every recipient must pass KYC with the Optimism Foundation, and if you do not pass, the grant returns to the round's treasury. Round 6 distributed 2.4 million OP for governance infrastructure, and the 2025 dev tooling round 8 million OP. Beautiful, large-scale, and completely locked against me, because my "did not pass KYC" is not about money. It is about the fact that I have no passport their verification provider is willing to look at.

Third: the story of [Gitcoin and the Farsi-language smart contract course](https://news.bitcoin.com/gitcoin-deactivates-grant-for-farsi-speaking-communities-due-to-u-s-sanctions/) is perhaps the most honest document of this era. Gitcoin switched off the "Free Smart Contract Development Course for Farsi Speaking Communities" grant not because the teachers were on a list, but because the very idea of a teaching course for people from a sanctioned country created legal risk for the whole organization. Five years have passed since, and in 2026 I found no reason to think anyone has become more willing to take risk.

Fourth, and this is the one thing that genuinely delighted me. Bug bounties in crypto keep their own schedule. [Immunefi](https://immunefi.com/bug-bounty/immunefi/) states in its rules that a program is open only to people living outside regions restricted by OFAC or UN Security Council resolutions, and for Polygon [full KYC is mandatory](https://immunefi.com/bug-bounty/polygon/information/), passport, place of residence, invoice. But next to that sit programs with no KYC at all: Hermetica (updated February 12, 2026) states "No KYC information is required for payout processing", with a critical finding paid at 10 percent of funds at risk, capped at $100,000 with a floor of $20,000; IPOR (March 2026) pays 50 percent in USDC and 50 percent in token, up to $100,000, no KYC required. So there is a whole niche where you are paid for a hole you found in a contract, and nobody asks from which city you found it.

Fifth: the openness of this world is not stable, and that must be remembered every time someone says "well, there is DeFi, nobody interferes there". [Project Catalyst on Cardano](https://cryptoslate.com/cardanos-project-catalyst-is-changing-hands-and-the-pause-is-forcing-builders-to-face-a-brutal-funding-gap/), the program that distributed roughly $150 million, was effectively halted in February and March 2026: Fund15, with a budget of 18.5 million ADA, and Fund16 will not run "in the proposed form", the money returns to the treasury, applications people had prepared for months are left without an answer and without a date. This is not about sanctions. It supports a different thesis: in crypto your "open" channel depends on someone else's governance stability just as much as my channel depends on somebody else's regulation. Nobody is protected, I simply have a lower cut-off threshold.

## 9. Chain six: fund money, or why venture capital does not see me

I will be brief here, because the law has already said everything. On April 6, 2022 [E.O. 14071, "Prohibiting New Investment in and Certain Services to the Russian Federation"](https://ofac.treasury.gov/system/files/126/14071.pdf), was issued (87 FR 20999). Section 1(a)(i) prohibits new investment in the Russian Federation by US persons to the extent the Treasury determines, 1(a)(ii) prohibits the supply into Russia of any category of services the Treasury determines, and 1(a)(iii) prohibits a US person from any approval, financing, facilitation or guarantee of a transaction by a foreign person that would be prohibited for a US person. In the EU there is a ban on new investments in a long list of sectors, and the 19th and 21st packages added pre-authorization requirements for essentially any services to Russian state bodies. For me this means, to the letter: a fund with American LPs (and in crypto that is nearly all of them, from a16z to Variant) may not give me a cent, from no vehicle, neither through Cayman nor through BVI, if the object of the investment is located in Russia or serves a person in Russia. Not "does not want to". Not "afraid of reputational risk". May not.

Now add the professional deformation of the sector I chose. RWA is not "crypto" in its naive 2017 sense. It is an industry that runs on compliance as on oxygen. BlackRock's BUIDL, the flagship tokenized treasury fund, at launch in March 2024 carried a $5,000,000 minimum and a "US qualified purchaser" requirement. As of June 2026 it sits near $2.5 billion of distributed value across eight networks including Ethereum, Solana, Polygon, Optimism, BNB, Avalanche, Arbitrum and Aptos, and in November 2025 it was accepted as collateral on Binance ([summary](https://www.spotedcrypto.com/rwa-tokenization-market-size-2026-guide-2/)). Its entire architecture is a chain: eligibility check, whitelist, transfer agent, on-chain allowlist. Securitize, which services it, sells the very thing that excludes me as a product.

And that, probably, is the bitterest thing I understood this year. I do not merely live somewhere you cannot call. I chose a specialization whose essence is the correct address. I came to study tokenization because it is the one area of the crypto economy that deals with real rights, real money, real markets, and not with waiting for a bull. It turned out that at the technical core of this area sits the same mechanism that blocks me: the allowlist. RWA is the industry that invented how to write a right into a blockchain, and at the same time the industry that knows what "you are not in the allowlist" means, better than anyone.

## 10. Chain seven: talent scouting, the layer of the industry where I am already useful

I first took the question "how can I take part in talent discovery" as naive. Now I think it was the single genuinely smart question I have asked myself in two years, and I will explain why.

The industry in 2026 does not hire through an ATS in the classic sense. It hires through a network: other people's threads, other people's repositories, other people's audit reports, other people's pitches on a Demo Day. The data say the same thing: 395 percent growth in demand for web3 roles against 450+ applications per engineering opening, a shortage of Rust, audit and protocol-architecture specialists, and [a shortage of people who understand regulation](https://eakdigital.com/crypto-recruiting-how-web3-companies-hire-top-talent-2026/) as the hardest role type to fill. When there are more candidates than jobs, whoever *finds* candidates wins. When candidates are scarce, whoever can explain who they are wins.

What is available to me from Sevastopol in this scheme, in practice:

The scouting layer requires neither an account nor a legal entity. [Superteam Earn](https://solanacompass.com/projects/superteam) is 205,600+ registered members and 2,600+ sponsors posting bounties, grants and jobs, and the platform pays in USDC. [A 2026 detail I find delightful and slightly frightening](https://gigs.sh/p/superteam-earn): there is now a first-class API for autonomous AI agents, with tiers "AGENT_ALLOWED" and "AGENT_ONLY", a typical listing around 1,500 USDC, and a rule that an agent may do the work but "a human must claim the listing for payouts", because an agent cannot pass KYC. The industry pushed automation to the point where robots earn at hackathons and kept one single human restriction, and that restriction applies to me too: a payment can go only to a verified person in a permitted country. In me this produced a wild, almost literary joy. So the problem is not my qualification. The problem is the procedure.

The talent layer has one more property I underrated for a long time: it is not taxed by sanctions until it touches money. Writing breakdowns of other people's repositories, translating and annotating research, producing tokenization market overviews for non-English audiences, finding people who do strong work and cannot talk about it, is activity that cannot be blocked because there is nothing to attach the block to. I came to the conclusion that for the next year or two I should become not an applicant but a node. I am better at that than at coding.

## 11. The market growing under my feet while I look the other way

Now the part for which all these numbers are worth listing, and which should make it clear to a reader: my story is not about the absence of work. It is about work that is forbidden to me on one side of a border, while the market on this side is boiling and hiring almost nobody.

Global figures for context: the total value of tokenized assets in 2026 is estimated at [$18.5-31.3 billion](https://app.rwa.xyz/) depending on whether stablecoins are counted, tokenized treasuries have grown to $23.9 billion (up 150 percent year on year), on-chain private credit is $8.3 billion with 79.3 percent of issuance on Coinbase's Base, and the number of holders of tokenized assets passed 398 million, 63.6 percent of whom live in emerging markets. So globally this market is growing where I expected it to: at the periphery, among people without access to ordinary financial instruments. Except that me, who formally fits that profile, the system still will not let in: I cannot open an account, which means I cannot be issued a token.

Numbers for the Russian tokenized-asset market ([analytics from Central Bank data](https://xn--80a3bf.xn--p1ai/cfa_q1_2026.html) and [Kommersant](https://www.kommersant.ru/doc/8587893)). The total volume of outstanding digital financial rights at the end of March 2026: 1,375.4 billion rubles, a doubling in one quarter and a 4.5 times rise in a year. The number of active issues grew from 35 in the first quarter of 2023 to 1,512 now. Registered users of the platforms are approaching a million (per AKRA, 757,000 individuals at the end of 2025, up 171 percent in a year). Primary placements in 2025: 1.65 trillion rubles; 2024: 593 billion; 2023: 64 billion. In July 2026, 167.9 billion rubles were placed, up 18.1 percent on June, while the number of issues fell from 156 to 154: the average size is growing, not the activity. Structure: 98 percent of 2023-2025 volume is debt instruments similar to bonds, 55 percent of 2025 issues mature within one month, 66 percent in the third quarter of 2025, so the instrument became a way for banks to shuffle short liquidity. The five largest issuers take 80 percent of volume. Meanwhile only some 208 billion rubles are in circulation for real, per Cbonds, roughly 10 percent of everything ever issued: a market where assets live to maturity and is not a market in the sense of secondary liquidity (secondary turnover 42.9 billion rubles per quarter, 3.6 percent). Platforms: A-Token (78 issues, 54.8 percent of July volume), CFA Hub, Atomicbit, Tokenon, Sber, SPB Exchange, VTB. Sber reports 132 issues with 752.4 billion rubles of issuance for the first quarter of 2026, which is 5.2 times the entire volume of all such platforms in the same period of 2025.

And a legal framework you cannot fail to respect, because it is honest: [an RBC analysis of March 24, 2026](https://companies.rbc.ru/news/yK2UP4eRUd/tokenizatsiya-nedvizhimosti-v-rossii-pochemu-ryinok-gotov-ranshe-zakona/) explains why there is no direct real-estate tokenization in Russia and there will not be without changing the base norms: the entry in the state property register is primary, a token does not replace state registration, and the list of digital rights under 259-FZ does not include proprietary rights over real estate, so only indirect tokenization is possible (the right to claim money from a sale, as in the Samolyot issues through Atomicbit with a 50,000 ruble threshold). For comparison, in Kazakhstan since May 1, 2026 the AIFC regulator's rules on second and third type digital financial assets are in force and the qualification gap is closed, but there too the token certifies a claim, not a title.

Why am I writing this in an essay about Crimea? Because this is the only part of my "future in RWA" that sanctions do not touch at all. The Russian digital-rights market does not care whether they let me into a conference in Amsterdam. It needs people who can write smart contracts against the legal construction "digital right" and at the same time understand how a claim to transfer money differs from a claim to transfer a piece of paper. In a country with 1,512 issues and 98 percent debt there are few such people. Here my registration is not a violation. Here it is just a registration.

And here I got properly angry, because this is the only point in the essay where the injustice does not look like "the West closed the door" but like "the door is open, and behind it a very low ceiling". The domestic market does not pay $180,000, does not give you an arXiv paper and does not put your name in contributors. It gives you employment.

## 12. Relocation: the only unlock, and its price

Every chain above converges on one point, and I will not pretend I found a bypass: a person's jurisdiction is a variable that effort cannot replace. So it has to be replaced literally: the person must stop being "ordinarily resident" where he now is.

What that means in law, really, in the terms of US statute: the embargo is attached to territory and to ordinary residence, not to a passport. Once I stop living in Crimea and become a resident of a third country, I stop being an object of Part 589. I do not become American, I do not stop being Russian, I stop being a "person in the Crimea region". This is why all my friends who draw crypto salaries today do not live in Russia but in Serbia, Armenia, Kazakhstan, Georgia, the UAE, Turkey.

But in my circumstances there are two gaps nobody mentions in relocation chats.

The first gap: leaving is physically possible only through Russia. The only legal route from Crimea [into mainland Ukraine, described in a guide of March 9, 2026](https://ru.krymr.com/a/gayd-dlya-krymchan-bezopasnyy-vyyezd-na-materikovuyu-chast-ukrainy/33689762.html), is across the Kerch bridge into Russia, then into Kazakhstan, Armenia, Georgia, Belarus or Moldova, then into Ukraine, where you have to re-prove your Ukrainian-ness by hand, collecting a birth certificate, a copy of a parent's passport, a school diploma and "any Ukrainian documents that survived". Put simply: I get to choose between the country I live in and the country I am a citizen of by birth, but only through a third and a fourth.

The second gap: I have two states that may find my conduct impermissible, and they do not talk to each other. On the Ukrainian side there is the 2014 law on the rights and freedoms of citizens and the legal regime on temporarily occupied territory, which bans among other things economic activity and financial operations in the occupied area, plus Article 111-1 of the Criminal Code on collaborationism, whose fourth part defines the offence as "conducting economic activity in cooperation with the aggressor state" ([analysis](https://en.thepage.ua/experts/criminal-liability-for-collaboration-with-the-russian-federation)); the National Bank of Ukraine has forbidden Ukrainian banks from operating in Crimea for as long as the occupation lasts. The practice is alive: a lawyer's [list of criminal proceedings from 2024](https://kasyanenko.com.ua/en/publications/a-list-of-cases-falling-under-article-436-2-of-the-criminal-code-of-ukraine-by-attorney-dmytro-kasyanenko) shows that in real cases Crimean residents are charged under part 6 of Article 111-1 and Article 436-2 together. This is not a joke about "they will prosecute you across the contact line". It means I carry a legal tail twelve years long, and moving to Yerevan does not shorten it.

The third gap, which I will cover in one sentence because it is not about the industry but about life: military registration and the exit restrictions that come with it for a man of conscription age in Sevastopol in 2026 may turn out to be the heaviest stone of all, heavier than the sanctions. I will not give advice here, I have no right to. But ignoring this factor in any "I will leave" strategy is childish.

And still, in substance, relocation looks like this: legal residency anywhere outside Russia removes 90 percent of my problems, including the European crypto restrictions (remember that the exception in the Commission FAQ works for holders of a long-stay D visa with registration), including the payment channels and nearly every "eligibility" field in the forms. The remaining 10 percent is that you pay for that right with rent, a visa, taxes, severed connections and years.

## 13. Two timelines: mine and that of anyone without my line in the passport

People often ask me how big the difference actually is. "You are not exaggerating?" I tried to count honestly, without theatrics, because theatrics do not scale. Take two people with the same stack: one lives in Chisinau, Tbilisi, Belgrade or Almaty, the other in Sevastopol. Both write Solidity, both understand how an issuance is built on top of a legal construction, both would pass the same technical interview.

Step one, "make yourself a business". For him: registering a sole proprietorship or a d.o.o. in one or two working days, state duties around 100-300 euros, an accountant at 50-150 euros a month. For me: the option is "not here". If I want a Serbian d.o.o. I must physically arrive in Novi Sad or Belgrade, and before that legalize my stay, and for that, as I showed above, first obtain somebody else's residency. The price of my first step equals the price of the entire relocation: at minimum 3,000-6,000 euros for the first three months (rent deposit, translations, apostilles, a visa run, signature legalisation, nostrification if needed), while my income at the moment of moving drops to zero, because it drops exactly when I stop being "here" and have not yet become "there".

Step two, "open an account". For him: a passport, an address, fifteen minutes, sometimes fully online. For me: Georgian banks in 2026 do not open accounts for Russians remotely, Armenian banks require a proven center of interests, Kazakh banks require an IIN and a personal visit, and the British director verification fails on geolocation. The gap between "fifteen minutes" and "four months and 4,000 euros" is the sanctions premium on my location.

Step three, "get an offer". For him: the employer clicks "offer", sends the data to the EOR platform, the contract arrives by email, the first payment in two weeks. For me: the same employer is obliged to route this through the legal team, then an outside sanctions screening firm, then the bank, and all three answer "no" not because I am weak but because they have a fine in their history. The real time from interview to start date for my acquaintances from Crimea is not two weeks but eight to twelve months and one relocation.

Step four, "go to a hackathon and a conference". For him: a ticket at 150-400 euros, a Schengen visa in two or three weeks, EES takes his fingerprints at the border and he is inside. For me: a passport issued in Sevastopol is not accepted for a Schengen visa at all, and not accepted for US visas; and if the passport was issued in another region, the Sevastopol registration turns my application into a folder marked "check this one extra carefully". A slot in Astana or Warsaw means a wait of two weeks to nine months, a $185 fee, and another couple of thousand euros of logistics, because there are no direct flights from Sevastopol to Astana and that is its own odyssey of three days.

Step five, "the salary". We saw the ranges: mid $180,000-280,000 total, senior $250,000-400,000 total. In practice, Russian-speaking developers in crypto who live in legally hireable countries get $5,000-9,000 a month on contract (by the type of listing that sits in [who-is-hiring](https://github.com/rebase-network/who-is-hiring/issues/1281), where "senior smart contract RWA, remote, USD 5000+" is a floor, not a ceiling). My realistic $10,000-30,000 a year, earned at online hackathons, bounties and no-KYC grants, differs from his $60,000-120,000 a year not even by a multiple but by mode of existence: mine is payment for a one-off job with no relationship, his is a contract, seniority, visa history, pension contributions, the right to a mortgage, the ability to rent without prepaying a year.

Here is what I want a reader to understand after those five steps. The difference between us is not competence. The difference is one variable, which he filled in by default at birth, and which is available to me only at the price of emigrating. In *Roadside Picnic* the Zone gives away for free what does not exist outside it, and entry is open. Mine is the reverse: entry is open, and everything inside costs twelve years of a life, because you are not allowed to take anything out.

## 14. What I will not do, and why this is not moralizing

This section is necessary, because in the comments under my posts it arrives first: "use somebody else's passport", "register a sole proprietorship in Kazakhstan by power of attorney", "use a proxy and do not show your face", "everyone writes about these restrictions to scare you, in practice everybody does it".

No.

Look at the actual cases, not at a Telegram thread. On June 10, 2025 the US Department of Justice [charged Evgeny Gugnin](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/founder-cryptocurrency-payment-company-charged-evading-sanctions-and-export-controls), founder, president, treasurer and simultaneously "compliance officer" of Evita Investments and Evita Pay. Per the indictment, through a crypto payment business he laundered more than $500 million by serving customers whose funds sat in sanctioned Russian banks (Sber, Sovcombank, VTB, Tinkoff), and for that he systematically lied to banks and exchanges that he did not work with Russia. The charges include evasion of sanctions, evasion of export controls, fraud against financial institutions, and Bank Secrecy Act violations. This is precisely the "scheme" that is offered to me as a "normal way to get paid", performed by a man who became a defendant.

Second example: Bittrex, $24 million, for *not interfering* with people from Crimea using the platform. Not for helping anyone bypass anything. For not blocking.

There is one more example, and as a developer it is the most unpleasant, because it is not about money but about code. Tornado Cash: in August 2022 OFAC put the mixer's smart contracts on the SDN list, and from that minute an American was forbidden even to interact with addresses on a blockchain. The developer Alexey Pertsev in the Netherlands was sentenced on February 27, 2025 to 64 months in prison, and note the wording: not for writing code, but for participation in a criminal organization that facilitated money laundering. The co-founder Roman Storm was found guilty by a jury in August 2025 of conspiracy to operate an unlicensed money transmitting business, and only half a year later, in February 2026, a second jury acquitted him on the count of conspiracy to evade sanctions; on March 9, 2026 the Justice Department moved for a new trial on the first count. His partner Tjwan van Loon remains a fugitive. A Dutch director made a documentary about Storm, released in 2025, and it contains a line worth an entire legal memorandum: "I should not have had to ask who uses my code". I will not build on tools that turn me into a defendant in someone else's jurisdiction. Not from fear, but because my only asset is the reputation of an engineer who does not create problems for a counterparty.

Now estimate what happens to a small crypto company that hires me and pays me under my Kazakh address while I actually sit in my flat in the Gagarin district with an IP that matches nothing. The first casualty is not me. It is the person who trusted me: they will be charged with "reason to know" and facilitation, and I get a permanent blacklist status in an industry that already treats me as a high-risk geography. There is a third layer people forget: since September 1, 2026 Law 282-FZ is in force in Russia, and by one legal analysis (from Pravorub) a peer-to-peer purchase with a Russian card is already interpreted as a violation for both sides, with blocks via Rosfinmonitoring lists, and from September 1, 2027 prohibitions are added that will turn that interpretation into the letter of the law. So the "gray" chain has become gray on both sides at once, and the grayness is no longer shared equally.

And there is a fourth layer, specifically Crimean: any scheme where I "hide" my place of residence is automatically a scheme in which I *admit* guilt before both jurisdictions. From the Ukrainian side, as I showed above, economic activity in cooperation with the aggressor state is Article 111-1, and if I start lying about my address to American providers, I simultaneously generate material that can be read as "he knew what he was doing", only aimed the other way. In this city you are not allowed to be careless: carelessness here is always read as a position.

So my principle is written in one paragraph: I do only what is legal *both* on their side and on mine. Anything that requires another person to break the law so that I can work is not an opportunity. It is somebody else's crash, and I am being invited into the passenger seat.

## 15. One percent: what I am doing, and why this is not a motivational post

I promised a conclusion in the spirit of "yes, it is brutal, 99 percent is closed, but the 1 percent is mine". Here it is, but with arithmetic, because "1 percent" without an estimate is a slogan.

My one percent is not "breaking the blockade". It is a set of four tracks, each of which has confirmed legality, an entry price and a ceiling. I have deliberately ordered them by decreasing probability of money and increasing probability of freedom.

### Track 1. The domestic digital-rights market as cash flow (my income estimate: 1.5-4 million rubles a year, entry in 1-3 months)

The argument is simple: 1,512 active issues, 13 operator platforms, 98 percent debt, and an absence of people who understand both Solidity and 259-FZ. Issuers do not need "blockchain evangelists", they need people able to design a smart contract around a specific digital right, around disclosure requirements, around the offer, around the register, and to explain to a lawyer where the economic meaning is lost in the code. This is work for which my address is not a violation: the client is Russian, the platform is Russian, the regulator is Russian, the payments are in rubles. Yes, there is no $250,000 here. What there is here pays for everything else, and this track must always stay switched on, because it is what buys me time.

### Track 2. The public layer: X, writing, GitHub (the money is not here, but every door opens here)

A separate note on Twitter, because it is the only channel where the sanctions regime does not work as a filter. The industry has no LinkedIn of its own and no ATS I could be admitted to; it has a feed, and in that feed there is no "country" field. This moves me to a childish delight, honestly.

At the same time I keep no illusions and I record the facts, so that nobody sells me "monetization". X stopped accepting new participants into Creator Revenue Sharing on August 7, 2026, the program closed on September 7, 2026 and was replaced by Original Content Rewards (applications from September 8). The old terms were: Premium, 500 verified followers, 5 million impressions in the verified home timeline over 90 days, a $30 minimum, biweekly payout, and a mandatory Stripe account in a supported country. Stripe is not in Russia, and even less in Crimea. So feed monetization is unavailable to me, and that is not a problem I will solve with somebody else's passport.

Why X then. Not for the payouts, but for three things that money cannot buy:

1. A direct channel to whoever decides on hiring, bypassing HR, the ATS and the policy. In crypto a founder reads a feed, not a candidate in Workday. When the founder of an RWA project sees a person who has spent a year explaining how tokenization works, he writes first. That is not naivety, that is literally how protocols staffed up in 2021-2023.
2. A portfolio that cannot be rejected over an address. The qualification "he is from Crimea" can only be overridden by the qualification "he did this, and this, and this, publicly, with open code and texts".
3. A niche where I am a monopolist. People who write about RWA do not know what it is to pass KYC in Sevastopol. People who know do not write about RWA. I do not need to compete with the best writers in the world, I need to be first in a category that does not exist: "building and tokenizing under a sanctions embargo applied to you personally". It is narrow, it is boring to 90 percent of readers, and it is precisely that which makes me citable for the remaining 10 percent. The format is deliberately odd: not "10 hacks" but documentary prose with a register of refusals, dates, order numbers and live irritation inside. A text like that is either read to the end or not opened; the middle is not provided for, and that is good, because the middle does not produce inbound requests.

### Track 3. International competitions and no-KYC bug bounties (ceiling $10,000-100,000 per event, cycle of 1-2 months)

Here everything is pragmatic: DoraHacks and similar venues with no country list in the rules, prize pools from $25,000 to over $120,000, paid in crypto, entered from home. A separate line, if you have a taste for reverse engineering: programs with no KYC such as Hermetica and IPOR, where a critical finding is 10 percent of funds at risk with a $20,000 floor. And one more, the most undervalued: open source and public goods, paid retroactively where KYC is not a condition (unlike Optimism, where it is). I deliberately do not include "just build a protocol and they will notice you": they will notice, but not everybody pays.

### Track 4. Changing residency (the main investment, and it pays back in access, not money)

The sequence I consider workable rather than fantasy.

Month 0-1. Reissue the foreign passport in another region of Russia (a document issued in Sevastopol is accepted neither for Schengen under Implementing Decision 2023/1061 nor for US visas). Assemble a "center of interests": a lease, certificates, statements, proof of income, Ukrainian documents if I have them and if I intend to use that track.

Month 1-3. First legal jurisdiction: Serbia (a d.o.o. plus privremeni boravak, duty 22,700 RSD, proof of about 1,500-2,000 euros in the bank, temporary residence up to three years, permanent after three), or Georgia (an IE at 1 percent, the virtual zone at 0 percent on exported IT services, but from March 1, 2026 a work permit is required, with a transition period to January 1, 2027), or the Kyrgyz option as a temporary bridge (per 2026 overviews, remote account opening for Russians has effectively survived only there). The selection criterion: where they will open an account for me *as a business*, because the account matters more than the company.

Month 3-9. Live there for real, because "ordinarily resident" is not a line in a form but a set of facts that get checked: tax residency, an address, 183 days of physical presence, and no activity from the old region.

Month 6-12. Open the UK Ltd through an ACSP, but now with real third-country residency and a clean geolocation; in parallel apply for a D visa where I intend to work (the European crypto block is removed only by legal EU residency, I remember the exception for holders of a long-stay D visa).

Month 9-18. Go for hiring. Not "into Coinbase", but into the role where my combination is unique: sanctions or compliance analyst, jurisdiction researcher, BD for CIS corridors, operations in stablecoin infrastructure. I am deliberately placing myself where knowing how the wall works is a qualification and not a defect. The 2026 industry, according to hiring reviews, is shortest of all in compliance and regulation: MiCA's transition ran to July 1, 2026, the American GENIUS Act of 2025 and the SEC documents that followed created a new layer of requirements, and people who understand sanctions screening not from a course but from their own experience of refusals are almost absent from the market.

Notice what emerged. My main asset in this industry is not Solidity and not two years of RWA research. My asset is twelve years of living inside a filtering system, which gave me an intuitive understanding of how an allowlist works on people. In a world where every second RWA issuance is an allowlist in a smart contract, I am in fact an expert on what happens to the ones who are not on it.

## 16. Instead of a conclusion: about the walls we chose

In *Notes from the House of the Dead*, Dostoevsky begins not with people but with a fortress wall: a yellow building, four wings, and he writes that the town in which they live resembles a fortress "which must be defended against the enemy". I always thought that was overdetermined, until I came to live in a city that is a fortress, except the defense works the other way round: from the inside you cannot get out to the world, from the outside nobody comes in, and nobody is defending anything, people simply stopped coming.

Camus, in *The Plague*, found the formulation that is more precise than any OFAC press release: a town is shut not because there is plague in it but because it is written in the papers, and all that remains is decency in small things, that is, going on doing your work as if nothing had happened. In the July 2026 economic report on Crimea from *The Insider* ([theins.ru](https://theins.ru/ekonomika/294575)) that decency looks like this: the port stands idle, there has been no foreign trade for twelve years, an entrepreneur drives across the bridge for petrol, an accounting firm cannot file its reports because the internet went down, and after the annexation the number of officials doubled in Crimea and tripled in Sevastopol. That, incidentally, is the real reason I will not find work here either: not only because they do not let me in there, but because the local economy has been taken apart for spare parts. According to [Forbes, citing the association of internet trade companies](https://www.forbes.ru/analiz/financy/534147), the number of small and medium businesses in both Sevastopol and Crimea in 2024 was lower than in 2021, and sanctions are named as the cause.

I am not going to write about *The Shawshank Redemption*. It ends well for reasons that have nothing to do with my reality, and at my exit there is not an ocean but a border, a checkpoint regime and a form.

Calvino is closer to me, with his *The Baron in the Trees*, where Cosimo refuses to touch the ground and lives his whole life in the trees without leaving the town that accepted him. He did not become free, he became alive in a different way: the same territory, another scale of height, and one day this became a form other people wanted to enter.

If you like, I can name it differently. In *Death Stranding* the world is broken into isolated towns whose inhabitants have forgotten how to visit each other, and the protagonist is neither a warrior nor a genius, he is a porter who hauls other people's cargo between the fragments of infrastructure. I like this role. I do not need to be admitted somewhere; I need to be a person through whom something passes: other people's code, other people's texts, other people's money, other people's reputation, my translations, my analysis, my hackathon pitch.

The summary I wrote for myself, and which can probably be argued with. Ninety-nine percent of standard doors are closed to me, and that is not a metaphor but the result of rereading twelve user agreements, four sanctions packages and one Devpost list of excluded countries. The remaining one percent looks like this: public work that cannot go unnoticed, competitions where nobody asks for an address, grants without KYC, a domestic market where my registration is not an anomaly, and, as the load-bearing structure, a change of residency, because until that variable is changed none of the others matter. That is little compared with a person from Chisinau, and it is the maximum compared with yesterday's me, who thought the whole thing was about the portfolio.

In *Golden City* by Grebenshchikov there is a song about a city "where we never are", and that, essentially, is the whole plot. I am not going to argue with the fact that I am never there. I am going to arrange it so that this city sometimes looks in here itself, because here there is something it does not have: a person who read every restriction line by line and is still building something.
